## Where the damages questions arise

A personal injury case converts a physical event into a set of claims, and the
largest of those claims are usually about work. What could the plaintiff earn
before the collision, the fall, or the incident? What can the plaintiff earn
now, within the restrictions the physicians have documented? Who performs the
household work the plaintiff used to do, and what would replacing that labor
cost? If care will be needed for years, what will it consist of and what will
it cost? None of these questions answers itself, and none of them is answered
credibly by advocacy. Each has a discipline responsible for it, a methodology
behind it, and published data against which any opinion can be checked.

## Earning capacity, before and after

The core vocational question in a personal injury case is the difference
between two earning capacities: the one the plaintiff had before the injury and
the one that remains after it. The evaluation starts with the record rather
than the claim. Education, training, and employment records establish what the
plaintiff did and what it required. Wage documentation establishes what the
work actually paid. Medical records, functional capacity evaluations, and
examining physicians' opinions establish the restrictions the analysis must
respect; the vocational expert adopts those restrictions as foundation and
identifies whose opinions they came from rather than creating medical limits
of his own.

From that foundation, a transferable skills analysis codes the pre-injury
occupations to the occupational taxonomies maintained by the United States
Department of Labor and compares their demands against the documented
restrictions. The result is a defensible list of occupations that remain
within capacity, if any, together with the wages those occupations command in
the relevant labor market under the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational
Employment and Wage Statistics program and state workforce data. Pre-injury
and post-injury earning capacity are stated in figures a reader can trace to
their sources, because in litigation the figures will be read by someone whose
job is to find the weak step in the chain.

## Household and family services

Earning capacity is not the only labor a person contributes. Cleaning,
cooking, yard work, home maintenance, child care, and care of other family
members all have measurable value, and an injury that limits them creates a
real economic loss even when wage loss is small. The vocational analysis
documents the scope of that loss: which services the plaintiff performed
before the injury, at what frequency, and which are now limited or lost,
cross-referenced against the medical restrictions so the claimed limitations
match the clinical record. Published national time use data provides
benchmarks for typical service hours. The conversion of documented service
losses into dollar values is economic work, and at Purinton Analytics it is
performed and signed by an independent economist partner using the vocational
findings as stated inputs.

## Future care needs

When injuries are severe, the largest single element of damages is often the
cost of future care. A life care plan sets out the anticipated medical care,
therapies, medications, equipment, supplies, and attendant services over the
duration of need, each item tied to a medical foundation in the record or to
the recommendations of treating and consulting physicians. Where the future
care question is narrower, a medical cost projection may fit the case better
than a full plan, and part of an honest intake process is saying which scope
the record supports. Costing draws on published and verifiable pricing sources
for the plaintiff's geography, and the plan is written so that every line item
can be examined, priced, and cross-examined on its own foundation.

## Role boundaries

Damages testimony is most useful when each expert stays inside a defensible
lane. The vocational expert does not opine on fault, on the medical cause of
the injuries, or on prognosis; those belong to liability experts and
physicians. The vocational expert does not compute present value or total
damages; those calculations are performed and signed by independent economist
partners coordinated by the firm. And no expert at the firm predicts what a
jury will award for pain and suffering, because that is not a question
methodology can answer. Keeping the boundaries clean is not a formality. It is
what allows each opinion to survive the scrutiny the other side is paid to
apply.

## For plaintiff counsel and for defense counsel

The same toolkit serves both sides of the caption. Plaintiff counsel typically
retain the firm to establish the existence and size of a loss: to document
restrictions in vocational terms, quantify the capacity difference, and found
a care plan the economist partner can cost. Defense counsel typically retain
the firm to test what has been served: whether the opposing vocational report
adopted restrictions from the record or assumed them, whether claimed wage
losses match published labor market data, whether the life care plan's items
carry medical foundation, and whether mitigation was reasonable. The firm
takes both kinds of engagements on the same terms, applying one methodology
whichever question is asked, which is precisely what makes the resulting
opinions useful in front of a judge applying Federal Rule of Evidence 702 or
its state analogue.
