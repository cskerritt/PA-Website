## Why malpractice damages are a coordination problem

Medical malpractice litigation splits naturally into two halves. Liability is
about what happened in the operating room, the emergency department, or the
chart. Damages are about what the patient's life will require from now on and
what has been lost. When the outcome is catastrophic, the damages half is
rarely one question. It is three questions stacked on top of each other: the
care the patient will need, the cost of that care over a lifetime, and the
earning capacity the injury forecloses. Each question belongs to a different
discipline, and the answers have to fit together without gaps and without
double counting.

That fit is where damages cases are won and lost. A life care plan that
assumes attendant care around the clock while the vocational report assumes a
return to full-time work is a gift to the cross-examining lawyer. So is an
economic analysis that prices a care item the medical record never mentions.
Purinton Analytics exists to prevent those seams: the firm's principal
performs the life care planning and vocational evaluation, medical cost
projections are built on physician foundation, and the economic analysis is
performed and signed by an independent economist partner working from the same
documented record. One engagement, one consistent set of assumptions, three
separately signed and separately defensible opinions.

## The life care plan and its foundation

The life care plan is usually the largest damages document in a catastrophic
malpractice case, and it is only as strong as its foundation. The plan
organizes anticipated needs into standard categories: physician follow-up,
therapies, diagnostics, medications, supplies, durable medical equipment and
replacement cycles, home and vehicle modifications, attendant and skilled
care, and case management. Each significant item is tied to the medical
record or to an identified recommendation from a treating or consulting
physician, because medical necessity is a physician's opinion, not a
planner's. Pricing uses verifiable sources appropriate to the patient's
geography, documented so that any line can be checked. Methodology follows
the published standards of practice for life care planners, and the plan is
written in the expectation that a capable opposing expert will read every
line.

## The vocational dimension

Catastrophic malpractice outcomes usually change what the patient can do for
work, and sometimes end it. The vocational evaluation documents the pre-event
profile from education, training, and employment records, adopts the
functional restrictions established by physicians and neuropsychologists, and
analyzes what employment, if any, remains realistically available and what it
pays under published labor market data. Where the patient is a child or had
not yet entered the workforce, the analysis follows the firm's pediatric
earning capacity methodology and says so candidly. The vocational findings
then become stated inputs to the economist partner's loss calculations,
keeping the earning capacity opinion and the valuation opinion in separate,
properly credentialed hands.

## The economic layer

Present value, growth and discount assumptions, worklife adjustment, and the
valuation of household services are economic questions. At Purinton Analytics
they are answered by independent economist partners: credentialed forensic
economists who perform and sign their own analyses using the life care plan
and vocational report as documented foundations. The firm coordinates
scheduling, record flow, and consistency of assumptions, and never presents
economic opinions as its own. Counsel receive one coherent damages package in
which each opinion is signed by the right kind of expert.

## Plaintiff and defense engagements

The firm accepts malpractice engagements from both sides of the caption. For
plaintiff counsel, the coordinated engagement builds the affirmative damages
case: a plan with clean foundation, vocational findings tied to the record,
and economics that survive scrutiny. For defense counsel, the same disciplines
run in reverse: an item-by-item audit of the served life care plan against the
medical record, a test of the vocational report's restrictions and wage data,
a review of the economic analysis for double counting and unsupported growth
assumptions, and a realistic statement of what the record actually supports,
which is often the most valuable settlement document a defense team can hold.
The methodology does not change with the client, and that is the point.

## Process and timing

Malpractice damages work follows the firm's standard sequence: documented
conflict check before any substantive discussion, scope definition with a
records request built around the injury, evaluation and analysis, then
reports delivered against the disclosure calendar with deposition and trial
support thereafter. Two timing realities deserve emphasis. First, life care
planning depends on physician foundation, and obtaining it is far easier
while discovery is open, so damages experts should not be the last retained.
Second, when a served plan or analysis needs rebuttal on a compressed
schedule, the firm takes rush review engagements when the record can
responsibly support them, and says no when it cannot.

Coordinated engagements also produce an artifact that separately retained
experts rarely have: a documented set of shared assumptions. The restrictions
adopted, the sources relied on, the care items carried from plan to economics,
and the vocational findings supplied to the economist partner are all stated
in writing across the reports. When deposition questions probe whether the
experts talked to each other and about what, the answer is on the record and
consistent, because the coordination itself was designed to be examined.
