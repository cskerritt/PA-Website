## Vocational questions inside a statutory system

Workers' compensation is a creature of statute, and every state builds its
own machine: its own definitions of disability, its own benefit formulas, its
own procedures for disputing them. Federal programs administered through the
Department of Labor's Office of Workers' Compensation Programs add further
frameworks of their own. What the systems share is a dependence on vocational
facts. Whether a worker can return to the employment held at injury, whether
other suitable employment exists within documented restrictions, what that
employment pays compared with the pre-injury wage, and whether retraining
would restore earning capacity are questions no statute can answer about a
particular worker. They require evaluation, and the quality of that
evaluation often decides the contested issues in the claim.

Purinton Analytics provides workers' compensation vocational evaluations

## The evaluation sequence

The analysis begins where every credible vocational opinion begins, with the
record. The job at injury is documented from the employer's job description
and, just as importantly, from how the work was actually performed, because
the physical demands of a title and the physical demands of a particular
loading dock are not always the same. Medical restrictions are taken from
treating and examining physicians and from functional capacity evaluations
where they exist; the vocational expert adopts restrictions with their
sources identified and does not create them. A structured interview covers
work history, education, skills, and the worker's own account of function.

The comparison of demands to restrictions answers the first statutory
question in most systems: whether the worker can return to the employment at
injury, with or without modification. When the answer is no, a transferable
skills analysis identifies the occupations the worker's history and education
support within the documented restrictions, coded against the occupational
taxonomies maintained by the United States Department of Labor. The wage
question is answered from published data, principally the Bureau of Labor
Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program at the state
and metropolitan level, supplemented by state workforce agency data.

## Labor market surveys

Many contested claims turn not on theory but on whether suitable work exists
in fact. A labor market survey addresses that question directly: it
identifies employers and openings for the occupations within capacity in the
relevant market, documents wages and physical demands, and reports findings
with sources that opposing counsel can check. Surveys serve both directions.
For an employer or insurer, a documented survey can substantiate that
suitable employment is genuinely available. For a claimant, a survey
conducted with the same rigor can demonstrate that the theoretical jobs in an
opposing report are scarce, geographically unrealistic, or inconsistent with
the actual restrictions. The firm conducts surveys with the same protocol
regardless of who asks.

## Modified duty, retraining, and feasibility

Systems generally favor return to work, and disputes often concentrate on
whether a specific path is realistic. The firm evaluates proposed modified
duty against the documented restrictions and the genuine content of the job,
distinguishing real accommodation from accommodation on paper. Where
retraining or a rehabilitation plan is proposed, the analysis addresses
feasibility in concrete terms: the worker's measured academic skills, the
entry requirements and completion rates of the specific program, its cost and
duration, and the documented wages of the target occupation. A plan that
survives that examination is worth funding; one that does not is worth
knowing about early, whichever side the finding favors.

## Boundaries and testimony

The firm does not provide impairment ratings, which are medical
determinations, and does not opine on compensability, disability categories,
or entitlement under any particular act, which are legal questions. Where a
claim requires economic projection beyond the vocational findings, the
analysis is performed and signed by independent economist partners
coordinated by the firm. What the firm provides is the vocational evidence
the statutory questions consume: employability, wages, market availability,
and feasibility, documented to publication standard. Evaluations and
testimony are available nationwide, in person or by remote appearance where
the tribunal permits, and the engagement process runs through the same
documented conflict check that begins every matter the firm accepts.

## When compensation claims travel with other cases

Work injuries frequently generate parallel proceedings: a third-party action
against an equipment manufacturer or a negligent driver, a long-term
disability claim, or a Social Security disability application, each applying
its own standard to the same underlying medical record. Inconsistent expert
work across those proceedings is a cross-examination gift, because a
vocational finding served in one forum is discoverable and quotable in the
next. The firm approaches parallel matters with one documented factual
record and clearly stated, standard-specific questions, so that an
employability conclusion under a compensation act and an earning capacity
opinion in the companion tort case rest on the same evidence and diverge
only where the governing questions genuinely differ, with the divergence
explained in the reports rather than left for opposing counsel to exploit.
