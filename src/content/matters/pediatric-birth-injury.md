## The hardest damages question in litigation

Pediatric injury cases, and birth injury cases above all, present the damages
question in its most demanding form. The plaintiff has no earnings history, no
work record, sometimes no school record, and a future that would have been
uncertain even without the injury. Yet the law requires a reasoned answer:
what would this child probably have earned over a working lifetime absent the
injury, and what capacity remains? An expert who claims certainty about a
two-year-old's career is not credible. An expert who refuses to analyze the
question at all leaves the jury with nothing but sympathy and speculation.
The defensible path runs between those failures: a documented methodology
that uses the best available evidence, states its assumptions, and
acknowledges its limits.

Purinton Analytics provides that methodology nationwide for plaintiff and
defense counsel, alongside pediatric life care planning and coordinated
economic analyses performed and signed by independent economist partners.

## Building the uninjured baseline

The first half of the analysis reconstructs the earning capacity the child
would probably have developed absent the injury. Because the child cannot
supply a work history, the analysis assembles the evidence that decades of
published research connect to adult attainment. The child's own record comes
first where age permits: developmental milestones, early academic
performance, standardized achievement results, and teacher observations.
Family attainment carries substantial weight for younger children, because
parental education and occupation are among the strongest documented
predictors of a child's eventual educational attainment. Federal statistical
sources then translate probable educational attainment into earnings
evidence: the National Center for Education Statistics on educational
pathways, and Bureau of Labor Statistics and Census Bureau series linking
educational attainment to median earnings and labor force participation.

The output is deliberately framed as evidence-based capacity, not prophecy.
The analysis identifies which conclusions rest on the child's documented
characteristics, which rest on family attainment, and which rest on
population statistics, so the trier of fact can weigh each layer for what it
is. That candor is not a concession. It is the methodology's armor.

## Measuring what remains

The second half addresses the child's post-injury capacity, and here the
clinical record does more of the work. Neuropsychological and
psychoeducational evaluations, therapy records, individualized education
programs, and physician statements of impairment establish the functional
picture. The vocational analysis translates those findings into work terms:
whether competitive employment is probable, whether supported or sheltered
employment is the realistic ceiling, whether earning capacity is reduced or
eliminated, and how the documented impairments interact with the entry-level
labor market as described by the Department of Labor's occupational data. For
children, this analysis often must be revisited as development unfolds, and
the report says so rather than pretending a snapshot is a lifetime.

## Life care planning for a lifetime measured in decades

Pediatric care plans differ from adult plans in duration, in developmental
staging, and in the role of caregivers. Needs change as the child grows:
therapies intensify and taper, equipment is outgrown and replaced on
documented cycles, school-based services end at defined ages and must be
replaced, and parental caregiving that is feasible for a small child becomes
attendant care for an adult. The firm's pediatric life care plans schedule
those transitions explicitly, tie each item to physician or clinical
foundation, and price against verifiable sources for the family's geography.
The economic valuation of the plan, including present value over a lifetime
horizon, is performed and signed by an independent economist partner.

## Plaintiff and defense engagements

Plaintiff counsel typically retain the firm to build the affirmative damages
case: baseline capacity, residual capacity, and a care plan that will be read
line by line by a capable opposing expert. Defense counsel retain the firm to
test served opinions: whether a claimed baseline outruns the family and
developmental evidence, whether the residual capacity opinion ignores
documented function, whether the care plan's items carry medical foundation,
and whether the economic analysis compounds optimistic assumptions. The
methodology is identical in both directions. In a field where sympathy runs
high and records run thin, disciplined method is what both sides are actually
paying for.

## Process and timing

Engagements begin with a documented conflict check, followed by a
pediatric-specific records request. Evaluations are scheduled around the
child's clinical calendar, in person or remotely as protocols permit, and
reports are delivered against the disclosure schedule. Because children
change, the firm flags when an updated evaluation before trial would
materially strengthen the record, for either side, and coordinates timing
with the independent economist partner so the complete damages package stays
consistent from foundation to valuation.

One further dimension deserves early attention in pediatric matters: the
household itself. A parent who leaves the workforce to provide care, or whose
hours and advancement contract around therapy schedules, represents an
economic reality that the governing jurisdiction may treat in different ways.
Where counsel determines that such losses are compensable, the vocational
documentation of the caregiving parent's history and capacity follows the
same methodology described above, and the valuation belongs, as always, to
the independent economist partner. Raising the question at intake costs
nothing; discovering it after disclosure deadlines costs a claim.
