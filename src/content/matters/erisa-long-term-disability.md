## Disability by definition

Long-term disability disputes are unusual among vocational matters because
the controlling standard is written in the policy. One claimant is disabled
if unable to perform the material duties of their own occupation; another is
disabled only if unable to perform any gainful occupation; and the same
claimant may pass from the first definition to the second at a transition the
plan sets, commonly after twenty-four months of benefits, though the period
is whatever the policy says it is. Wage thresholds, national economy
language, and occupation definitions vary from plan to plan. A vocational
analysis that ignores those definitions answers the wrong question, and in
this field the wrong question is fatal, because the decision-maker, whether
an administrator or a court, is bound to the definitions in the document.

Purinton Analytics prepares vocational analyses addressed to the policy
language actually at issue, nationwide, for claimants building a record and
for insurers and plan administrators evaluating one. In ERISA-governed
matters the firm's work is shaped by a further structural fact: review is
frequently confined to the administrative record, so the analysis has to be
in the file, complete and well-founded, before the administrative process
closes.

## The own-occupation analysis

The own-occupation question begins with an accurate account of the
occupation. Policies commonly define it as the occupation as performed in the
general or national economy, not the particular job at the particular
employer, and the difference matters in both directions: a claimant whose
employer added heavy physical duties to a normally sedentary occupation, or
one whose light job title concealed genuinely heavy work, will be
misclassified by a review that never looked past the title. The firm
documents the occupation both ways, as performed and as generally performed,
using the Department of Labor's occupational resources to establish the
material duties, exertional level, and cognitive demands of the occupation in
the general economy. Documented restrictions from the medical file are then
compared against those demands, duty by duty, and the opinion states whether
the material duties can be performed, which duties fail, and on what
documented restriction each failure rests.

## The any-occupation and gainful analysis

At the any-occupation stage the field widens but the discipline tightens. A
transferable skills analysis identifies the occupations the claimant's
education, training, and work history support within the documented
restrictions. Each candidate occupation is then tested against the policy's
definition of gainful, which typically sets an earnings threshold tied to a
percentage of pre-disability earnings or an indexed amount. That test is
arithmetic, not adjective: the analysis documents the wages of each candidate
occupation in the relevant labor market from the Bureau of Labor Statistics
Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program and shows whether the
threshold is met. Occupations that exist in trivial numbers, or that no
longer exist as the file's dated resources describe them, are flagged. The
result is an opinion that either identifies genuinely gainful occupations
within capacity or demonstrates, with cited data, that none exists.

## Reviewing the file's vocational reasoning

Many engagements in this area are reviews of work already in the file:
occupational classifications, transferable skills screens, and labor market
findings produced during claim handling. The firm reviews that material the
way opposing counsel eventually will. Was the occupation classified from the
actual duties or from a title? Are the cited occupational resources current,
and do they still describe the occupation as it exists? Were restrictions in
the medical record carried into the vocational reasoning, or silently
dropped? Do the wage figures meet the policy's gainful threshold when the
arithmetic is actually run? The review reports what holds and what does not,
in either direction, and the firm accepts these engagements from claimants
and from plans on the same terms.

## Boundaries, process, and forums

The firm's role stays vocational. Plan interpretation, the applicable
standard of review, and preemption questions belong to counsel and the court.
Medical restrictions are adopted from the clinical record with their sources
identified, never created. Benefit arithmetic and offsets are policy
accounting outside the vocational lane, and any broader economic projection a
matter requires is performed and signed by independent economist partners
coordinated by the firm. Engagements begin with a documented conflict check,
proceed on a schedule built around administrative deadlines, and conclude
with reports written for the record. Where the forum permits testimony, the
firm appears by declaration, deposition, or hearing, in person or remotely,
anywhere in the country.

## Disability files rarely travel alone

Most long-term disability claimants also pursue Social Security disability,
and many policies require it. The two systems ask related but different
questions under different definitions, and a file that contains a Social
Security decision, favorable or not, contains vocational evidence that the
disability insurer's reviewers and the courts will read. The firm's analyses
account for that companion record explicitly: where the standards align, the
consistency is documented; where they diverge, the divergence is explained in
terms of the differing definitions rather than ignored. An analysis that
addresses the whole evidentiary landscape is harder to discount than one
that pretends the parallel proceeding does not exist.
