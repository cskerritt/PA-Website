## A vocational question inside a ratings system

The VA rating schedule assigns percentages to conditions, but a percentage is
not an answer to the question that matters most to many disabled veterans:
can I still work? Total disability based on individual unemployability
exists for exactly that gap. Under 38 CFR 4.16, a veteran whose
service-connected disabilities prevent securing and following a substantially
gainful occupation may be compensated at the total disability rate even when
the schedular ratings do not reach 100 percent. The regulation's percentage
thresholds in subsection (a) and its extraschedular pathway in subsection (b)
frame the eligibility, but the core finding, unemployability by reason of
service-connected disability, is a vocational finding.

That is where the claims process often runs thin. Rating decisions are built
on medical examinations that describe impairment; far fewer files contain a
professional analysis of what the documented impairments mean for actual
employment, for this veteran, with this work history, in the real labor
market. Purinton Analytics prepares that analysis: independent vocational
evaluations addressed to the regulatory standard, nationwide, for accredited
representatives, attorneys, and the veterans they serve.

## Addressing the regulatory standard, not a proxy for it

A useful TDIU opinion answers the regulation's question in the regulation's
terms. Substantially gainful occupation is analyzed against its recognized
boundary: marginal employment, which the regulation ties to earned income at
or below the poverty threshold for one person and extends to protected
environments where earnings alone overstate true competitiveness. The
evaluation therefore does not stop at whether any job is imaginable; it
addresses whether work the veteran can secure and follow rises to the
substantially gainful level, with earnings evidence from published federal
wage data. Only service-connected disabilities drive the analysis. Where
non-service-connected conditions exist, the evaluation separates their
effects with the same care an opposing reviewer would demand, because
conflating them is the fastest way to make an opinion useless.

## Methodology adapted to the veteran's file

The evaluation follows the firm's standard vocational sequence, adapted to
the VA context. Records review covers rating decisions, service treatment
and VA medical records, Compensation and Pension examination findings on
occupational functioning, and any Social Security disability determinations,
which frequently contain vocational evidence of their own. The structured
interview documents the military occupational history and its civilian
translation, post-service work attempts and why they ended, education and
training including service schools, and the veteran's account of current
function. A transferable skills analysis codes the work history against the
Department of Labor's occupational taxonomies and tests remaining occupations
against the documented service-connected limitations. Labor market and wage
findings come from published federal data, so that the line between
substantially gainful and marginal employment is drawn with evidence rather
than adjectives.

Failed work attempts and sheltered arrangements receive particular
attention. A veteran who has cycled through short-lived jobs, or who holds a
position preserved by a sympathetic employer's accommodations well beyond
what the market would offer, presents an employment record that can be read
superficially against the claim. The evaluation reads it properly: as
evidence about the ability to follow employment over time, which is part of
the regulatory standard itself.

## Where the opinion fits in the process

The firm's vocational opinions are prepared for submission by the veteran's
representative in claims and appeals, including proceedings before the Board
of Veterans' Appeals, and for extraschedular referral support where the
subsection (a) percentages are not met. When a file already contains
employability conclusions, the firm reviews whether that evidence addressed
the regulatory standard, adopted the documented limitations, and used
occupational data honestly, and responds where it did not. The firm does not
opine on service connection, nexus, or ratings, does not provide legal or
procedural advice, and does not promise outcomes. Where a companion civil
matter requires economic loss analysis, that work is performed and signed by
independent economist partners coordinated by the firm, keeping the TDIU
opinion itself strictly vocational. What the firm provides is what the
adjudicator otherwise lacks: a documented, methodologically grounded answer
to the vocational question at the center of the claim.

## Access and engagement

TDIU work is accepted nationwide. Evaluations are conducted remotely under
appropriate protocols or in person when the referral questions require it, a
practical point for veterans far from metropolitan areas. Engagements begin
with the firm's documented conflict check, records are gathered against a
checklist specific to VA matters, and completed opinions are delivered to
the representative for the record. The same evaluation, addressed to the same
standard with the same method, would read no differently if the requesting
party changed, and it is written on that assumption.

Many veterans pursuing TDIU also hold or seek Social Security disability
benefits, and the two systems evaluate work capacity under different
definitions: Social Security considers all impairments, while TDIU turns on
service-connected disabilities alone. A file containing both proceedings
contains potentially divergent findings that an adjudicator must reconcile.
The firm's evaluations address the companion record directly, explaining
consistency or divergence in terms of the differing standards, so the
vocational opinion helps the reader make sense of the whole file rather than
adding one more unreconciled document to it.
