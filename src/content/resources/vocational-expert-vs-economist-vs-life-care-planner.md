## Three questions, three disciplines

Most injury damages presentations answer three questions. What can this person
still do for work, and what does that work pay? What will their injury-related
care cost over a lifetime? And what are those future losses worth today? Each
question belongs to a different discipline with its own methodology, its own
credentialing, and its own signature. Cases fail on this point in a
predictable way: an opinion crosses a boundary, and the crossing becomes the
center of the challenge rather than the merits.

## Role comparison matrix

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Vocational expert | Life care planner | Forensic economist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core question | What work remains within capacity, and what does it pay? | What future care is needed, and what does it cost? | What are the losses worth in present value? |
| Typical inputs | Medical restrictions, education, work history, wage records, labor market data | Medical records, treating recommendations, therapy and equipment records, cost research | Vocational findings, life care plan costs, earnings and benefits records, statistical tables |
| Typical outputs | Employability, placeability, earning capacity, and labor market access opinions | A costed plan of future medical and support needs with clinical foundation | Present value of earnings losses, household services, and future care costs |
| Signs | The vocational evaluation report | The life care plan or medical cost projection | The economic damages report |
| Credentials commonly held | CRC, CVE, ABVE Fellow or Diplomate | CLCP, often with rehabilitation or nursing background | Advanced degree in economics; forensic economics practice |
| Testimony | Deposition and trial on vocational opinions | Deposition and trial on care needs and costs | Deposition and trial on valuation |

## Who signs what

The signature rule is the simplest test of a damages lineup. The vocational
expert signs opinions about employability, placeability, earning capacity, and
labor market access. The life care planner signs the plan of future care and
its costs, each item traced to a clinical foundation. The economist signs the
present value calculations, the growth and discount assumptions, and the
valuation totals. Physicians sign the medical opinions everyone else builds
on: diagnoses, prognosis, restrictions, and life expectancy where it is
disputed.

When a report offers an opinion its author will not be qualified to defend,
counsel on the other side has an obvious line of attack, and counsel on the
offering side has a problem that no rebuttal can fully repair. Checking
signatures against disciplines before disclosure costs nothing.

## How the roles sequence

The dependency order is fixed by the logic of the analysis. The vocational
expert and the life care planner work from the medical record and can proceed
in parallel once restrictions are documented. Their outputs, earning capacity
findings and costed future care, become stated inputs to the economist. The
economist then applies worklife expectancy, growth, and discounting to produce
present value figures.

Practical consequences follow. Retain all needed disciplines early, under one
conflict check, even though the economist's active work comes later. Give the
economist a defined delivery date for the upstream reports. And leave room in
the schedule for each expert to be deposed on their own piece after
disclosure.

## When you need one, two, or all three

A wage loss claim with a documented return to lighter work may need only the
vocational expert, with economic work added if future losses must be reduced
to present value. A catastrophic injury with lifetime care needs typically
requires all three disciplines, coordinated so the assumptions line up. A
medical cost dispute without an earnings claim may call for a life care
planner or a medical cost projection alone. Review and rebuttal engagements
mirror the same map: match the reviewing discipline to the discipline of the
report under review.

The same framework serves defense counsel evaluating an opposing lineup: which
questions does the case actually present, which disciplines were disclosed,
and where did any opinion cross a boundary it cannot defend.
