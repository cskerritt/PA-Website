## How to use this worksheet

Print this page and fill in the dates for your case. Start with the scheduling
order: its dates control over everything else on this page. Where the order is
silent and the case is in federal court, the Rule 26(a)(2)(D) defaults noted
below apply. State court deadlines come from state rules and the court's
orders. When the key dates are in, work backward through the retention timeline
to set target dates for referral, records, and evaluation.

| Milestone | Where to find it | Your date |
|---|---|---|

| Rebuttal expert disclosures | Scheduling order, or the federal default below | |
| Close of fact discovery | Scheduling order | |

</div>

Two entries deserve special attention. The expert-challenge motion deadline
determines when opinions must be fully defensible on the record, and the close
of fact discovery limits how long the records base can keep growing.

## Federal default timing

Absent a stipulation or a court order, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure
26(a)(2)(D) supplies the defaults: expert disclosures are due at least 90 days
before the date set for trial or for the case to be ready for trial, and
disclosures intended solely to contradict or rebut another party's expert
disclosure are due within 30 days after that party's disclosure. Most cases
are governed by a scheduling order instead, and many orders set earlier dates
than the defaults.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Disclosure | Federal default when no order controls |
|---|---|
| Principal expert disclosures | At least 90 days before trial or the trial-ready date |
| Rebuttal expert disclosures | Within 30 days after the other party's expert disclosure |

</div>

## Working backward: a retention timeline

The lead times below are working guidance, not rules. Actual timing depends on
records volume, provider response times, scheduling, and the scope of the
referral questions; the firm confirms realistic dates at intake.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Working milestone | Typical position before the disclosure deadline | Your target date |
|---|---|---|
| Conflict check submitted and cleared | As early as the damages theory takes shape | |
| Retention and scope call completed | 10 to 14 weeks or more for a full evaluation | |
| Tailored records request issued | Immediately after retention | |
| Core records received by the expert | 6 to 8 weeks before disclosure | |
| Evaluation or assessment completed | 4 to 6 weeks before disclosure | |
| Draft analysis complete, gaps identified | 2 to 3 weeks before disclosure | |
| Final report delivered to counsel | Ahead of the disclosure date, with margin | |

</div>

The pattern to notice: the expert's writing time is rarely the constraint.
Records collection is. Every week added on the front end reduces the pressure
on the back end.

## Sequencing a multi-expert engagement

In a coordinated damages engagement, the economist's analysis depends on
inputs from the other disciplines, so the sequence matters:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Retain the team at the same time, under one conflict check, even though the work sequences</li>
  <li>Vocational evaluation and life care plan proceed first, in parallel where the records allow</li>
  <li>Earning capacity findings and plan costs then pass to the independent economist partner as stated inputs</li>
  <li>The economic analysis is completed and signed by the economist, with time reserved before the disclosure date</li>
  <li>Leave room after disclosure for expert depositions before any motion deadline</li>
</ul>

Add roughly two to four weeks to the single-expert timeline for the economist's
work after the upstream reports are complete, and more where plan costing is
extensive.

## Rush and rebuttal timelines

Rebuttal engagements run on the compressed 30-day rhythm of the federal
default or on whatever window the order provides. They are more feasible on
short notice than full evaluations because the opposing report defines the
file: send the report, its appendices and data, the author's CV, and any
deposition transcript. For imminent disclosure, deposition, or trial deadlines,
call rather than write; the firm will say quickly whether the date can be met
responsibly.

## Common scheduling pitfalls

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Retaining the expert after fact discovery closes, leaving no mechanism to obtain the records the analysis needs</li>
  <li>Assuming state court practice mirrors the federal defaults</li>
  <li>Disclosing before core records arrive, then depending on supplementation that opposing counsel will challenge</li>
  <li>Leaving no gap between disclosure and the expert-challenge motion deadline for depositions</li>
  <li>Sequencing the economist first, before the vocational and life care planning inputs exist</li>
  <li>Treating a moved trial date as moving every expert date without checking the order</li>
</ul>
